# How Do You Size Battery Energy Storage Systems for a Commercial Facility

Sizing a Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) for a commercial facility is not simply a matter of choosing the largest battery that fits the budget. The right system must match the facility’s electricity consumption, peak demand, operating hours, backup requirements, solar generation, and future energy needs.

For businesses in Chennai and across Tamil Nadu, getting this calculation right can make a meaningful difference. Commercial electricity demand can vary throughout the day, particularly in manufacturing units, IT offices, hospitals, warehouses, hotels, and shopping centres. A battery system that is too small may not deliver the expected savings, while an oversized system can increase the initial investment without providing enough additional value.

The practical approach is to start with the facility’s actual electricity data, identify the problem the battery is expected to solve, and then calculate the required power and energy capacity.

This guide explains how businesses can approach BESS sizing, what information they should collect, and which local operating conditions in Chennai should be considered. ## **1. Start by identifying what the battery needs to do**

Before calculating battery capacity, a business should be clear about why it needs energy storage. Different applications require different system sizes, even when the facilities have similar electricity consumption.

For example, a manufacturing unit in Ambattur may want to reduce short periods of high electricity demand caused by machinery starting together. A hospital may need stored energy to support selected critical equipment during an interruption. An office building on OMR may want to store surplus rooftop solar electricity and use it later in the day.

These are different operating requirements, so they should not automatically use the same battery configuration.

The most common commercial applications include:

**Peak demand reduction:** The battery supplies part of the facility's electricity during periods of high demand. This can reduce the maximum grid demand recorded by the meter, depending on the tariff and billing method.

**Solar energy storage:** The battery stores surplus electricity produced by rooftop solar panels during the day and releases it when the building needs power later.

**Backup power:** The battery supplies selected loads when the grid is unavailable. The system must be designed around the equipment that genuinely needs backup, rather than assuming the entire facility must remain operational.

**Energy time shifting:** The battery charges during a lower-cost or high-solar-availability period and discharges during a more expensive or operationally important period. Whether this saves money depends on the applicable tariff and the system's round-trip efficiency.

A facility may want to achieve more than one of these objectives. However, the designer must establish which application has priority because peak shaving, backup reserves, and solar shifting can compete for the same stored energy.

## **2. Collect the facility's actual electricity consumption data**

The most important step in BESS sizing is understanding how the facility uses electricity throughout the day.

A monthly electricity bill provides useful background, but it is not enough to determine the correct battery size. Two businesses can consume the same number of units per month and still have very different demand patterns.

One facility may operate machinery continuously at a relatively steady load. Another may use large amounts of electricity for only a few hours, creating sharp demand peaks. Their storage requirements will be different.

Ideally, collect 15-minute or 30-minute interval electricity data for at least several weeks. A longer period, such as 6 to 12 months, is more useful because it can reveal seasonal changes, production cycles, holidays, and unusual operating days.

The information should include:

- Maximum demand recorded during each billing period.
- Average and peak electricity consumption.
- Electricity use during daytime, evening, and night shifts.
- Operating hours and production schedules.
- Solar generation, if rooftop solar is already installed.
- Existing generator or UPS operation.
- Important loads that must remain powered during an outage.

If interval data is unavailable, a facility can begin with meter readings, energy bills, and temporary load monitoring. However, a final investment decision should ideally be based on measured load data rather than estimates alone.

For a factory in Guindy or Sriperumbudur, the assessment should also consider whether machinery starts simultaneously, whether compressors operate in cycles, and whether production shifts change during the week.

## **3. Understand the difference between kW and kWh**

This is one of the most important concepts in battery sizing.

A BESS has two main capacity measurements: power and energy.

**Power capacity, measured in kilowatts (kW),** tells you how much electricity the battery can deliver at a given moment.

**Energy capacity, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh),** tells you how much electricity it can store and deliver over time.

For example, a battery rated at 100 kW and 200 kWh can theoretically deliver 100 kW for two hours, before accounting for usable capacity, conversion losses, and operating limits.

The basic relationship is:

A business that needs to reduce a short demand spike may need a high-power system with relatively modest energy capacity. A business that wants to store solar electricity for several evening hours may need more energy capacity.

This is why selecting a battery based only on the facility's monthly electricity consumption can lead to an unsuitable design.

## **4. Calculate the required battery power for peak shaving**

If the main objective is reducing peak demand, the first calculation is the difference between the facility's existing peak demand and its desired grid-import limit.

For example, imagine a commercial facility in Chennai has a measured peak demand of 500 kW. The management team wants to limit grid demand to 350 kW during a particular operating period.

The initial battery discharge power requirement is therefore 150 kW.

This is a simplified example. In a real project, the designer must also consider the facility's load changes, inverter rating, motor-starting requirements, power factor, and whether the battery can deliver the required output continuously.

The target should be selected after reviewing the actual demand curve. Reducing the peak by a large amount may require a substantially bigger system, so businesses should compare several possible demand targets rather than choosing an arbitrary figure.

## **5. Calculate how much energy the battery needs to store**

Knowing the required power is only half the calculation. The battery must also have enough energy to sustain that output for the required duration.

Suppose the facility needs the battery to deliver an average of 150 kW for two hours.

The facility therefore needs 300 kWh of usable energy delivered to the AC load during that period.

However, a battery's advertised capacity is not always the same as the amount of electricity that can actually be used. The usable capacity depends on the permitted depth of discharge, conversion efficiency, operating conditions, and battery ageing.

For illustration, assume:

- Required AC energy: 300 kWh
- Permitted usable discharge fraction: 90%
- Discharge-path efficiency: 95%
- Planned end-of-life capacity fraction: 80%


A preliminary design might therefore consider a battery around 440 kWh or larger, subject to the manufacturer's specifications and a detailed engineering assessment.

These percentages are illustrative assumptions, not universal battery specifications. Actual values should come from the proposed battery system's datasheet, warranty, and guaranteed performance at the expected operating temperature and discharge rate.

The calculation also assumes the battery must still meet the 300 kWh requirement at the planned end-of-life capacity. A project may use a different degradation strategy, such as scheduled battery augmentation, but that needs to be included in the financial model.

## **6. Size the inverter and power conversion system correctly**

A battery with sufficient energy capacity can still fail to meet a facility's needs if its inverter or Power Conversion System (PCS) is undersized.

The PCS controls the flow of electricity between the battery and the facility's AC electrical system. It must be capable of delivering the required power at the point where the facility needs it.

For example, if the peak-shaving calculation requires 150 kW of battery output, the system needs an appropriate AC power rating that can continuously support that requirement. The final rating may need to be higher after considering operating limits, reactive power requirements, temperature-related derating, and other equipment constraints.

The engineering team should also check whether the battery is expected to operate alongside rooftop solar, the grid, and an existing generator. Their controls must coordinate safely.

For facilities with large motors, pumps, lifts, compressors, or sensitive electronic equipment, starting current and transient power requirements deserve particular attention. A system designed only around average running load may not perform as expected when equipment starts.

## **7. Consider solar generation when sizing a commercial BESS**

Many businesses in Chennai are exploring rooftop solar to manage electricity costs. Adding battery storage can help use more of that solar generation beyond the hours when sunlight is available.

However, the solar system and battery should be sized together.

Consider a commercial building with a rooftop solar installation that produces surplus electricity during the afternoon. If the building's consumption is relatively low during those hours, some generated energy may be exported or otherwise not used on-site, depending on the system arrangement and applicable rules.

A battery can store some of that surplus energy and discharge it later, such as during the evening shift.

To calculate the required storage capacity, the designer should compare:

- Solar generation throughout the day.
- The building's electricity demand at the same times.
- The amount of surplus solar energy available for charging.
- The energy required after sunset.
- The battery's charging and discharging limits.
- Any grid-export restrictions or interconnection conditions.

A common mistake is to size the battery using the solar panel capacity alone. A 100 kW solar installation does not automatically require a 100 kWh battery or a 200 kWh battery. The correct storage capacity depends on the generation profile and the amount of energy the business actually wants to shift.

For a Chennai facility, the assessment should use site-specific solar generation estimates and actual consumption data. Cloud cover, monsoon conditions, roof orientation, shading, and seasonal changes can all affect available charging energy.

## **8. Calculate backup requirements separately**

Some businesses want a BESS mainly to maintain operations during power interruptions. In that case, the calculation should begin with a list of critical loads.

A hospital, for example, may need backup for selected medical equipment, emergency lighting, communication systems, and essential support infrastructure. A warehouse may need security systems, network equipment, selected lighting, and critical controls. A manufacturing plant may only need enough stored energy to complete a safe shutdown.

That is the required energy delivered to the loads before allowing for battery operating limits and conversion losses. The nominal battery capacity must be higher after those factors are included.

The designer must also check whether the BESS can operate in island mode, whether a suitable transfer arrangement is available, and whether the facility needs a grid-forming inverter or other equipment to establish a stable supply during an outage.

A grid-connected battery does not necessarily provide backup automatically. Some systems are designed to shut down when the grid fails unless the installation includes the necessary backup functionality and protection arrangements.

## **9. Account for Tamil Nadu electricity tariffs and demand charges**

For commercial facilities, the financial value of a battery depends on how electricity is billed.

Tamil Nadu's tariff structure includes different consumer categories and demand or fixed charges. The Central Electricity Authority's published tariff information for Tamil Nadu, effective from 1 July 2025, lists HT demand charges and energy charges for categories including industries, factories, IT services, and other commercial consumers. The applicable category and any subsequent tariff or subsidy changes must be checked against the latest official orders before preparing a business case.

This matters because a BESS designed to reduce peak demand may create value differently from one designed to shift energy use.

For example, a factory may have a short period of high demand that influences its billed maximum demand. A battery could discharge during that interval and reduce grid import. But the actual financial benefit depends on the meter's demand measurement interval, tariff provisions, recorded demand, and how consistently the battery can respond.

Businesses should not assume that every unit of battery discharge creates a saving equal to the full electricity tariff. Charging losses, battery degradation, maintenance, and the cost of charging energy must also be included.

Before finalising a BESS size, the energy consultant should model the proposed battery against the facility's actual tariff and billing data.

## **10. Consider Chennai's climate and site conditions**

Battery performance is affected by the environment in which the system operates. This is particularly important for commercial installations in Chennai and other parts of coastal Tamil Nadu.

High ambient temperatures can increase cooling requirements and affect battery ageing if the system is not properly designed. Coastal air can also create corrosion risks for exposed electrical equipment and metal enclosures.

The site assessment should consider the following factors.

**Temperature management:** Check the battery manufacturer's permitted operating temperature range and the cooling system required to maintain it. The battery enclosure should be installed in a location that avoids unnecessary heat exposure.

**Humidity and coastal conditions:** Enclosures, cable connections, fasteners, and supporting structures should be suitable for the site's humidity and potential salt exposure. The required ingress protection and corrosion protection should be selected based on the actual installation environment.

**Space and access:** Allow enough room for the battery cabinets, inverter, fire protection equipment, maintenance access, and emergency response. A compact layout should never compromise safety or servicing.

**Electrical protection:** The design should include suitable isolation, earthing, overcurrent protection, surge protection, monitoring, and emergency shutdown arrangements.

**Fire safety:** Battery chemistry, system design, installation location, ventilation, detection, suppression strategy, and emergency procedures should be assessed by qualified professionals in line with applicable standards and local authority requirements.

A battery system installed in an industrial site in Ambattur may face different environmental and operational conditions from one installed inside an office complex on OMR. The design should reflect the actual site rather than relying on a standard layout.

## **11. Choose a battery size that works over its full operating life**

Battery capacity changes over time. Repeated charging and discharging, temperature exposure, operating conditions, and calendar ageing gradually reduce the energy a battery can store.

This is known as battery degradation.

A system that meets a facility's energy requirement when new may not deliver the same amount of usable energy several years later. That is why a commercial BESS should be evaluated over its expected project life rather than only on its first-year performance.

When comparing battery proposals, businesses should review the guaranteed capacity retention, cycle-life conditions, warranty duration, permitted operating temperature, charging and discharging limits, and any restrictions on daily cycling.

It is also important to understand whether the supplier guarantees energy at the battery terminals or at the facility's AC connection point. Those figures are not necessarily identical because conversion and auxiliary equipment consume energy.

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's work on battery storage planning highlights the importance of matching power rating and energy capacity to the intended application. It also explains why oversizing can leave part of the system underused and tie up capital unnecessarily.

## **12. Compare different battery sizing scenarios before investing**

A business should not rely on a single proposed battery size without comparing alternatives.

For example, the same commercial facility might evaluate a smaller battery for short peak events, a medium-sized battery for peak shaving and limited solar shifting, and a larger battery for longer-duration energy shifting or selected backup loads.

Each option should be assessed against the same operating data and financial assumptions.

The comparison should include the expected reduction in grid demand, usable energy delivered, charging source, round-trip efficiency, expected annual cycles, battery degradation, installation cost, maintenance cost, and estimated savings.

It should also identify the conditions under which the battery will not be able to deliver the expected benefit. For example, a battery may not be fully charged before an evening peak if the facility has insufficient solar surplus or charging time.

The best system is not necessarily the one with the highest kWh rating. It is the one that meets the facility's operational objectives at a reasonable whole-life cost.

If the facility also wants backup power, that requirement should be added to the operating strategy. The battery cannot be assumed to provide the full peak-shaving energy and the full backup reserve simultaneously unless it has been sized and controlled to do both.

## **14. Common mistakes businesses should avoid when sizing a BESS**

Even a technically capable battery can deliver disappointing results if the original sizing assumptions are incorrect.

One common mistake is choosing a battery based only on the facility's monthly electricity bill. Monthly consumption does not show when demand peaks or how long those peaks last.

Another mistake is focusing only on kWh capacity and ignoring the inverter's kW rating. A large battery may still be unable to supply the required power if the PCS is undersized.

Some businesses also underestimate charging requirements. A battery must have enough time and available energy to recharge before the next planned discharge. If the facility expects to charge from rooftop solar, the solar generation profile must support that expectation.

Ignoring degradation is another costly oversight. A design should account for how much capacity will remain after years of operation, not just the battery's new-condition rating.

Finally, businesses sometimes expect a grid-connected BESS to function as an automatic backup system. Backup operation requires the correct electrical architecture, protection, controls, and load segregation. It must be included in the original design.
